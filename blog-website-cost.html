How Much Should a Small Business Website Actually Cost?

Website July 25, 2026
  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. How Much Should a Small Business Website Actually Cost?

You've gotten three quotes for a new website. One says $400. One says $2,000. One says $8,000. They're supposedly all building the same thing — so why is the gap that big?

It's a fair question, and most owners never get a straight answer. The truth is that "website" isn't one product with one price. It's a bundle of separate decisions, and each one moves the number. Once you know what those decisions are, you can tell a lowball quote that'll cost you later from a markup that isn't buying you anything extra.

How Many Pages You Actually Need

This is the simplest driver and the one most owners underestimate. A one-page site for a brand-new business costs far less to build than a multi-page site with a homepage, a services page for each service, a gallery, and individual location pages. More pages means more content to write, more images to source, and more time testing that everything works on a phone.

Before you get a quote, write down what pages you actually need. A massage therapist might need five. A contractor who does three very different services might need eight. Padding a site with pages you don't need just to look bigger online wastes money both up front and every time something needs updating later.

Custom Design vs. a Template

Think of it like buying furniture. A template is IKEA — it's assembled from parts someone else already designed, it's fast, and it's inexpensive. Custom design is a carpenter building a piece to fit your exact space. Both can look good. But only one is built around your business specifically, and that takes more hours.

A template-based site is a perfectly reasonable choice for a business just getting started. A custom-designed site earns its higher price when your brand, your layout needs, or your competition make "good enough" not good enough. Neither one is automatically the "right" answer — it depends on what you need the site to do.

  • Template: faster to launch, lower cost, limited to the structure it was built on
  • Custom: built around your specific services and photos, costs more, more flexible long-term
  • Either way: it should still load fast and work cleanly on a phone

A cheap website that has to be rebuilt in a year wasn't actually cheap. It just moved the real cost to later.

This is where a lot of "too good to be true" quotes fall apart. A $400 site that can't be edited, isn't built for search engines, or breaks on mobile isn't a bargain — it's a second project waiting to happen. A properly scoped website design should hold up for years, not months.

E-Commerce and Ongoing Maintenance

If you're selling products online, the price jumps again. Shopping carts, payment processing, inventory tracking, and shipping calculations are all separate pieces that have to work together reliably — unlike a simple page that just has to display information correctly.

And then there's the cost nobody mentions upfront: upkeep. A website isn't a one-time purchase like a sign you hang and forget. Software needs updates, images need refreshing, and if your hosting or security lapses, the whole thing can go down. A quote with no mention of what happens after launch is missing a real piece of the total cost.

So when you compare quotes, don't just look at the bottom line. Ask what's included: how many pages, whether it's custom or template-based, what happens if you need an e-commerce feature later, and who handles updates once it's live.
